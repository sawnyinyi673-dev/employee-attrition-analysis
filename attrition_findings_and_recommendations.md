# Employee Attrition — Findings and Recommendations

## Purpose

This note summarises what the attrition analysis found and points to the areas
worth attention. It does not prescribe an HR or compensation fix. The goal is to
show *where the problem sits* and *why*, so that the people who own retention
policy can decide how to act.

---

## What the analysis found

**1. Attrition is uniform across departments.**
Every department loses employees at roughly the same rate — between 19.5% and
20.5%. About one in five employees leaves, everywhere. This means the department
itself does not explain who leaves. A department-level view washes the signal out.

**2. The signal lives one level down, in pay.**
Inside every department, leaving is concentrated in the lowest-paid quartile
(the bottom 25% of salaries). The lowest-paid band leaves far more than the
highest-paid band, in every department without exception.

**3. It is pay, not simply tenure.**
The lowest-paid employees also tend to have shorter tenure, which raises a fair
question: are they leaving because of low pay, or just because they are
newer? To separate the two, employees were compared *within the same hire year* —
same time at the company, same experience level. The pattern held: in all 16
hire-year groups, the lowest-paid band still left more. So the effect is linked
to pay, not only to how long someone has been employed.

**One-line summary:**
Salary band and hire-year cohort answer *why* people leave (low pay, and not just
newness). Department answers *where* to act. All three matter, for different jobs.

---

## Areas worth attention

Because the departure rate is flat across departments, there is no single
"problem department." The problem is a pay level that exists company-wide. That
said, two different lenses point to two different sets of departments, and both
are worth a closer look.

### By severity — where the low-paid band leaves at the highest rate

Measured only within the lowest-paid quartile, these departments show the
sharpest departure rates:

| Department | Departure rate in lowest-paid band |
|---|---|
| Sales | 46.4% |
| Finance | 43.0% |
| Marketing | 41.8% |

These are the areas where the per-employee experience looks worst. The headcount
in these low-paid bands is relatively small, so the absolute number of leavers is
lower — but the intensity is highest here.

### By volume — where the most low-paid employees actually leave

Measured by the number of people leaving from the lowest-paid band, two large
departments dominate:

| Department | Low-paid leavers (approx.) | Share of all company departures |
|---|---|---|
| Development | 8,800 | ~15% |
| Production | 7,600 | ~13% |

Their departure rate is not unusual (around 38%, close to the company norm), but
because these are the largest departments, the same pay problem produces the
greatest number of actual leavers. Together, the lowest-paid band in these two
departments accounts for roughly **27% of every departure in the company**.

### How to read the two lenses

- **Severity lens (Sales, Marketing, Finance):** where the problem is most acute
  per person. Worth attention if the goal is to fix the worst individual
  experience.
- **Volume lens (Development, Production):** where attention would touch the
  largest number of people. Worth attention if the goal is to move the overall
  company attrition number.

Neither lens is "more correct." They answer different questions. Development and
Production are the priority for scale of impact; Sales, Marketing, and Finance are
the priority for depth of the problem in each affected employee.

---

## Two limits to keep in mind

**1. Salary is measured at different moments for leavers and stayers.**
For employees who left, salary is recorded at the point they departed. For
current employees, salary is recorded as of the latest date in the data.
Salaries rise over time, so leavers' salaries were frozen earlier and tend to
fall into lower pay bands for that reason alone. This means the size of the
pay effect is likely overstated. The *direction* of the finding is safe — low
pay is clearly associated with leaving — but the exact gap should be read as an
upper estimate, not a precise figure.

**2. This is an association, not a proven cause.**
The analysis shows a strong and consistent link between low pay and higher
attrition. It does not prove that raising pay would reduce attrition. The honest
conclusion is that low pay is worth investigating as a driver, ideally by testing
a targeted pay adjustment and measuring whether attrition falls — rather than
assuming the fix in advance.

---

## Bottom line

Attrition is a company-wide, pay-level problem, not a department problem. It
concentrates in the lowest-paid quartile everywhere. For scale, Development and
Production are where the most affected people are. For severity, Sales,
Marketing, and Finance show the sharpest rates in their low-paid bands. Both are
reasonable places for the retention owners to look next.
